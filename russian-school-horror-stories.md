# Russian School Horror Stories — With Actual Forum Sources

> **Note:** These are mostly first-person complaints and discussions from Russian sites such as Mail.ru Answers, Babyblog and 7ya.ru. They are anecdotes rather than independently verified court findings. What is often most revealing is not only the alleged incident itself, but how other adults react to it.

---

## 1. Students made to clean an entire school

A schoolgirl on **Mail.ru Answers** wrote that students washed the floors **every day after lessons**.

Then they were reportedly ordered to clean practically the **entire three-storey school — floors, walls and other areas**.

She said that during a previous cleaning session she developed a severe headache and asked the supervising teacher to leave. According to her, the teacher refused.

Instead of universally reacting with “why are schoolchildren doing the cleaners’ job?”, many adults responded along the lines of:

> “We cleaned schools too and survived.”

> “You won’t fall apart.”

> “Physical labor builds character.”

> “Several generations did this and nobody died.”

[Mail.ru Answers — students cleaning a three-storey school](https://otvet.mail.ru/question/98073957)

---

## 2. “You may not leave the school”

A student on Mail.ru described trying to leave during the school day because she urgently needed to go somewhere.

According to her account, the security guard physically blocked the door and demanded a written note from the class teacher.

The school effectively starts functioning like a controlled-access institution where entering is expected, while leaving requires authorization.

[Mail.ru Answers — security guard refusing to let a student leave](https://otvet.mail.ru/question/242811294)

---

## 3. A child asks to use the toilet, the teacher refuses — and he soils himself in class

One of the most extreme discussions appeared on **7ya.ru**.

A **second-grade child reportedly asked the teacher twice to go to the toilet**.

The teacher refused to let him leave.

Eventually, the child **could no longer hold it and soiled his pants right there in the classroom**, in front of the other children.

He then had to leave and clean himself while classmates laughed at him.

What makes the thread particularly striking is that even after an outcome this humiliating, the discussion still turns into an argument over whether the teacher had really behaved outrageously.

The familiar defenses appear:

> “Children should go during recess.”

> “They need to learn to wait.”

> “You cannot let everyone leave whenever they want.”

So an eight-year-old literally **shitting his pants because an adult refused him permission to use a toilet** can still be reframed as a lesson in discipline.

[7ya.ru — discussion about a child being refused permission to use the toilet](https://conf.7ya.ru/fulltext-thread.aspx?cnf=Educate&trd=17892)

---

## 4. School toilets without basic privacy

Russian parent forums also contain casual recollections of school toilets where stalls had **no doors at all**.

Some people describe renovated toilets having doors at first and those doors later being removed.

And once again, the reaction is frequently:

> “We had the same thing.”

> “Everyone survived.”

> “Children are too sensitive nowadays.”

Basic privacy becomes something that can be dismissed simply because previous generations were also denied it.

---

## 5. Sick children being prevented from going home

Students on Russian forums describe situations where they felt seriously ill but could not simply leave because the correct bureaucratic permission was missing.

One ninth-grader reported having a temperature of approximately **38°C** but being encouraged to remain at school.

Other students describe situations where the medical office was closed, yet teachers still refused to release them because they lacked formal authorization.

The logic becomes:

> **The child is obviously sick.**  
> **The person who can certify that the child is sick is unavailable.**  
> **Therefore the child must remain in school.**

---

## 6. School security acting like border control

Students regularly ask online whether school guards may physically prevent them from leaving school during the day.

One complaint describes a guard standing at the exit and demanding paperwork before allowing a student to leave.

The basic relationship becomes strangely custodial:

> **You are required to be there, and your ability to leave is treated as something requiring permission.**

[Mail.ru Answers — student prevented from leaving](https://otvet.mail.ru/question/242811294)

---

## 7. Teachers confiscating phones

Phone confiscation is an enormous genre on Mail.ru.

One student described a teacher collecting students’ phones. When the student claimed not to have one, another student identified him; according to the account, staff then found the phone and said it would only be returned to his mother.

[Mail.ru Answers — phone confiscated until parent arrives](https://otvet.mail.ru/question/239988261)

---

## 8. Going through students’ private messages

Another student alleged that a teacher took her phone, demanded access and started reading private conversations.

According to the student, this included **WhatsApp conversations with her mother**.

Other stories describe teachers allegedly opening Telegram, VK, galleries and other personal apps while searching for school-related videos or messages.

That is a very different issue from simply saying:

> “Put your phone away during class.”

It turns into:

> **“Give me access to your private communications.”**

[Mail.ru Answers — teacher allegedly reads private messages](https://otvet.mail.ru/question/235676089)

---

## 9. Phones confiscated even during recess

Another discussion concerns phones being collected not only during lessons but **during recess**.

Even some people who support banning phone use during lessons argue that confiscating them throughout the break is excessive.

Others defend it simply because school authority has announced the rule.

[Mail.ru Answers — phones confiscated during recess](https://otvet.mail.ru/question/239775942)

---

## 10. Long hair becomes an administrative issue

A male student asked whether a school principal could forbid boys from having long hair and threaten to **refuse admission to lessons** until they cut it.

Instead of hairstyle being an ordinary personal choice, it becomes a disciplinary matter for school administrators.

There are similar complaints about staff promising to find “means of influence” against boys refusing haircuts.

One particularly bizarre anecdote even involved school staff allegedly arguing that long hair could contribute to **strabismus** because it covered the eyes.

[Mail.ru Answers — threats over a boy’s long hair](https://otvet.mail.ru/question/54447158)

---

## 11. Girls being ordered to remove makeup

A sixth-grade girl on Mail.ru described her class teacher repeatedly making her remove makeup.

According to the student, she had previously been taken into an office and required to wash it off using **soap**.

When she asked whether this was normal, some respondents simply said yes.

The underlying principle becomes:

> **The teacher ordered it, therefore the order itself does not need justification.**

---

## 12. School uniforms as a tool of control

Students and parents complain about schools enforcing extremely specific clothing requirements.

Examples include girls being pressured to wear skirts rather than trousers even during cold weather.

Other complaints concern schools pushing uniforms from a particular supplier.

A basic dress code can therefore turn into regulation of very specific personal choices.

---

## 13. Children used as unpaid school staff

One Mail.ru student described school “duty” where pupils spent their recesses standing in corridors and **stopping other children from running**.

The same students reportedly had additional duties such as watering plants and feeding classroom animals.

So instead of having a break, they become:

- students,
- hall monitors,
- security assistants,
- maintenance workers.

---

## 14. Forced summer labor

Russian schools have a long tradition of summer “practice.”

Students describe being required to:

- clean school grounds,
- weed flowerbeds,
- wash furniture,
- move equipment,
- paint,
- perform other maintenance work.

One student complained that after missing summer practice, a teacher allegedly threatened to make her **work in the school cafeteria throughout the school year**.

The standard defense is:

> “It teaches children to work.”

> “They will appreciate the school more.”

> “We all did it.”

---

## 15. “Voluntary-compulsory” Saturday cleanups

Parents describe being asked to:

- wash windows,
- wash curtains,
- paint playground equipment,
- clean classrooms,
- perform maintenance.

Officially these activities may be voluntary.

But teachers may personally contact parents, distribute tasks or repeatedly ask why somebody did not participate.

This produces the classic Russian concept:

> **“Voluntary-compulsory.”**

Technically voluntary.

Socially mandatory.

[Babyblog — “voluntary-compulsory” parent labor](https://www.babyblog.ru/community/shkola/post/3218757)

---

## 16. Collective punishment

Students describe teachers punishing an entire class because several pupils behaved badly.

Examples include:

- keeping everyone after lessons,
- removing recess,
- assigning additional work,
- giving the whole class poor marks.

A student who did nothing wrong can still be punished because he belongs to the same group.

---

## 17. Academic grades used as punishment

Some students complain that teachers give **failing academic grades because of behavior**.

A “2” in the Russian grading system is supposed to mean failure to demonstrate knowledge.

But in these stories, it becomes:

> “You were noisy, therefore you get a 2.”

Sometimes even:

> “The whole class was noisy, therefore everybody gets a 2.”

The grade stops measuring knowledge and becomes a disciplinary weapon.

---

## 18. Medical exemption from PE that does not actually exempt you

Students with official medical exemptions from physical education report still being required to attend PE.

Some say teachers make them:

- sit through the whole lesson,
- bring sports clothes,
- perform “light” exercises,
- write essays for every lesson they cannot physically participate in.

So the exemption simply replaces physical exercise with another compulsory task.

---

## 19. Medical certificates after tiny absences

Parents and students complain about schools demanding medical paperwork even after very short absences.

A child misses one day.

The family may then be told to visit a clinic purely to obtain a certificate.

A healthy child ends up entering the medical bureaucracy simply because the school wants another piece of paper.

---

## 20. Mandatory assemblies before lessons

Students ask whether they must attend school assemblies that take place **before the official beginning of lessons**.

For example, a regular assembly might begin around 8:10 while the first lesson starts later.

Officially it may not be an ordinary lesson.

Practically, students can still face pressure for not appearing.

---

## 21. Paid excursions that somehow become compulsory

Students have asked on Mail.ru whether refusing to attend a **paid school excursion** can count as truancy.

This creates an absurd combination:

- the family has to pay,
- the event is not an ordinary lesson,
- yet refusing to purchase participation can still produce disciplinary pressure.

---

## 22. Class photographs treated as mandatory

Even something as trivial as a graduation or class photo album can become another conformity issue.

Students ask online whether they can refuse because teachers or classmates insist that **everyone has to participate**.

The question stops being:

> “Do you want a photograph?”

and becomes:

> “Why are you refusing to do what everyone else does?”

# The Parent Committee Universe

This is where the system becomes particularly strange.

Russian public education is formally free, yet some parent committees effectively construct their own parallel taxation system.

---

## 23. Parents paying for basic school necessities

Parents describe collections for:

- toilet paper,
- drinking water,
- cups,
- printer paper,
- soap,
- cleaning products,
- curtains,
- blinds,
- furniture,
- repairs,
- security,
- cleaners,
- workbooks,
- electronics,
- decorations,
- teacher gifts,
- children’s gifts,
- events,
- excursions.

The classic defense is:

> **“IT IS NOT EXTORTION. IT IS FOR YOUR OWN CHILDREN.”**

Other recurring responses:

> “Are you really too greedy to give 500 rubles?”

> “We have always paid.”

> “The government will not provide it anyway.”

> “Your child uses these things too.”

---

## 24. Toilet paper and drinking water paid for by parents

One Babyblog discussion describes a class fund used for:

- napkins,
- toilet paper,
- drinking-water coolers,
- other everyday supplies.

Separate money may still be collected for workbooks, gifts, excursions and cleaners.

A basic public institution therefore develops an additional privately funded operating budget.

[Babyblog — class fund, toilet paper and water](https://www.babyblog.ru/community/shkola/post/3234682)

---

## 25. “If you don’t pay, your child shouldn’t use it”

Arguments about class funds sometimes reach the point where adults discuss whether a child whose parents did not contribute should use supplies purchased by everyone else.

Toilet paper and drinking water become ideological questions.

The logic turns into:

> **“Your parents refused to pay, so why should you benefit?”**

[Babyblog — cooler / toilet-paper dispute](https://www.babyblog.ru/community/mnogodet/post/3222171)

---

## 26. Public lists of parents who did not pay

One mother complained that the parent committee published a **list in the common class chat showing who had paid and who had not**.

The discussion then included reminders asking where the missing money was.

Thus:

> **Payment is officially voluntary.**  
> **Refusal is legally possible.**  
> **But everybody will know that you refused.**

[Babyblog — public list of non-paying parents](https://www.babyblog.ru/community/shkola/post/3220057)

---

## 27. Children punished because their parents did not pay

This is perhaps the ugliest part.

Parent discussions seriously contemplate children of non-paying families receiving:

- no New Year gift,
- no sweets,
- no paid excursion,
- no supplies bought from the class fund,
- or visibly less than their classmates.

The child becomes leverage against the parent.

---

## 28. Children excluded from celebrations

One discussion concerns children whose parents had not contributed money for holiday gifts.

The proposed solution was essentially:

> **Do not bring those children to the celebration**, because otherwise it would be awkward when everyone else received a present.

[Babyblog — non-paying families and New Year celebrations](https://www.babyblog.ru/community/school/post/1704923)

---

## 29. Sending a child away while everybody else receives gifts

Adults have recalled childhood experiences where, because their parents had not contributed, they were **sent out of the room while classmates received presents**.

The disagreement is between adults.

The humiliation is imposed on the child.

---

## 30. “Your parents didn’t pay, so don’t be upset”

In discussions about gifts, people openly defend telling children something equivalent to:

> **“Your parents did not pay for your gift, so do not be upset that you get nothing.”**

Sometimes this is presented as simply teaching children how “real life” works.

---

## 31. Punishing a family until they start paying

One Babyblog commenter proudly described a family being excluded from various collectively funded things after refusing to pay.

The following year, she noted, that mother became **the first person to contribute money**.

The exclusion was presented almost as a successful behavioral correction technique.

[Babyblog — family excluded from gifts, excursions and materials](https://www.babyblog.ru/community/mnogodet/post/3137739)

---

## 32. Parent committees behaving like tax authorities

Some committees maintain records of payments, chase non-payers and announce collection deadlines.

They may effectively say:

> “We decided to collect X rubles.”

Even when no meaningful vote ever occurred.

The parent committee becomes a tiny unofficial government.

---

## 33. “A decision has been made”

Parents complain about committees simply announcing:

> **“A decision has been made.”**

Nobody remembers voting.

A few active parents decide what everyone supposedly needs, and the rest are expected to comply.

---

## 34. No financial reporting because “we trust them”

Some parents explicitly say they never ask for detailed reports on class-fund spending.

Their explanation:

> “We trust them.”

Questioning where the money went can itself make someone look antisocial or difficult.

---

## 35. People who object become outsiders

Parents describe disagreeing with collections or committee initiatives and then being:

- attacked,
- ignored,
- labelled problematic,
- removed from group chats.

Thus an unofficial volunteer committee can become a gatekeeper to the class community.

---

## 36. “Homework” actually completed by parents

Teachers assign elaborate crafts, models, posters, costumes and presentations that young children obviously cannot produce alone.

Parents spend evenings completing the assignment.

Everybody knows adults did much of the work.

Yet the fiction remains that these are children’s school projects.

---

## 37. Teachers selling paid extra lessons

There are also allegations from students that teachers sell private lessons to their own pupils.

One Mail.ru student alleged that a teacher who was also a deputy principal assigned material during paid extra lessons and then gave poor marks to students who had not attended those lessons.

This is an individual allegation rather than a proven general practice, but it illustrates the conflict created when a teacher controls both grades and access to paid instruction.

---

## 38. Public humiliation justified by good academic results

Parents sometimes discuss teachers who yell at children, publicly shame them or otherwise humiliate them.

The defense can be:

> “But she teaches very well.”

> “She made them disciplined.”

> “Their grades improved.”

The implicit principle becomes:

> **If the academic results are good, humiliation was worth it.**

---

# The Recurring Mentality

Across cleaning, toilets, phones, money, illness, appearance and discipline, the same arguments appear again and again:

> **“We did it too and survived.”**

> **“Nobody died.”**

> **“It builds character.”**

> **“They need discipline.”**

> **“Don’t stand out.”**

> **“The teacher is always right.”**

> **“Why do you know your rights so well?”**

> **“Everyone else pays.”**

> **“Everyone else obeys.”**

> **“Why should your child be special?”**

> **“It is for your own good.”**

The recurring pattern is:

> **arbitrary authority + collective pressure + petty bureaucracy + humiliation + unpaid labor + informal payments + people defending all of it because previous generations endured it too.**

The institution does something unreasonable.

Somebody objects.

And instead of asking whether the rule itself makes sense, a large part of the discussion becomes:

> **“Why can’t you just obey like everybody else?”**
